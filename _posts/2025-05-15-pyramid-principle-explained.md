<h2>🛕 What is The Pyramid Principle?</h2>
<p>The Pyramid Principle is a technique for writing and communicating complex information to stakeholders. Barbara Minto created it while working at McKinsey in the 1970s and 1980s.</p>
<p>The pyramid is a visual representation of how your ideas and insights fit together.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-overview.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1201" alt="Diagram of the Pyramid Principle: a Main Takeaway at the top, three Key Insights below it, and a group of data and information beneath each insight" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>The Pyramid is broken down into three levels:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Bottom level:</strong> This is your data, information and research.</li>
  <li><strong>Middle level:</strong> The key insights and summaries based on your data/information.</li>
  <li><strong>Top level:</strong> The main takeaway you communicate to stakeholders from lower levels.</li>
</ul>
<p>For decades, the billion-dollar strategy and management consultancy market (BCG, McKinsey, and Deloitte) has used it to communicate complex ideas to busy business executives.</p>
<p>It's used to persuade leadership teams at companies to invest in business strategies.</p>

<h2>🤷 Why use The Pyramid Principle?</h2>
<p>The Pyramid Principle is effective and popular because it's based on how the human mind works.</p>
<p>The human brain has evolved to impose order on the world around it, and one of its core capabilities is to recognise patterns (<a href="https://fortelabs.com/blog/a-pattern-recognition-theory-of-mind/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a>).</p>
<p>As we take in ideas one after another, our brain's pattern recognition feature assumes they belong together. And tries to turn them into a logical pattern we can understand (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_recognition_(psychology)" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a>).</p>
<p>Any human mind will group information based on similar characteristics or distance. For example, take a look at these six dots:</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-six-dots.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1334" height="516" alt="Six grey dots arranged as three pairs, with a gap between each pair" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>Your mind will automatically group these six dots into three groups of two because of their distance. This is your pattern recognition core functionality in action.</p>
<p>But where does the pyramid structure come from?</p>
<p>Well, according to Barbara Minto, pattern recognition in the human mind will always be a pyramid because of three factors:</p>
<ol>
  <li><strong>📦 The Magical Number Seven</strong> - The average number of items and chunks of information that can be held in short-term memory.</li>
  <li><strong>🔂 The Logic of Relationships</strong> - The relationship between categories and items must be clearly stated.</li>
  <li><strong>⏬ Ordering from the top-down</strong> - The clearest communication presents information from the top down.</li>
</ol>

<h3>📦 The Magical Number Seven</h3>
<p>Anyone's ability to process information in their short-term memory is limited.</p>
<p>How much information can you process?</p>
<p>According to George A. Miller, an American psychologist at Harvard in 1956, the number of objects an average person can hold in short-term memory is 7 (plus or minus 2). Some people can remember 3 or 5 items, while others can remember as many as 9.</p>
<p>This short-term memory limit is called the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magical_Number_Seven,_Plus_or_Minus_Two" target="_blank" rel="noopener">magical number seven, plus or minus 2</a>.</p>
<p>What is interesting about George A. Miller's research is that if the mind processes more items than it can handle, it begins to group information into logical categories (<a href="https://labs.la.utexas.edu/gilden/files/2016/04/MagicNumberSeven-Miller1956.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a>).</p>
<p>Let's look at an example.</p>
<p>You have a shopping list with 10 items. Your mind will automatically group items on the list into logical categories based on location in the supermarket:</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-magical-number-seven.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1310" height="1201" alt="The Magical Number Seven: a ten-item shopping list grouped into three categories, Dairy (milk, eggs, butter, yoghurt), Fruit (apples, carrots, bananas) and Veg (carrots, potatoes)" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>These categories and the items beneath them form the pyramid structure. They form a pyramid because the items in your memory are grouped under each category. And you remember them in order (one after the other).</p>
<p>The shopping list is easier to remember because of the pyramid structure.</p>

<h3>🔂 The Logic of Relationships</h3>
<p>It's not just about grouping items together to make remembering easier.</p>
<p>The relationship between categories and items must be clear. The mind will expect the items within the group to be characterised based on the category name.</p>
<p>For example, take the shopping list. The categories should be self-explanatory. They are grouped based on the location within the store, but it's always important to state it clearly. The mind will be able to spot out-of-place items based on the category name:</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-logic-of-relationships.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1000" alt="The Logic of Relationships: Dairy selection, Fresh fruit and Fresh vegetables groups are ticked, while a Fresh meat group is crossed out because it contains pasta and rice" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>It's not just the category-item relationship that needs to be clear. The relationship between the categories also needs to be clear.</p>
<p>If the sequence of categories isn't clear, it can be hard for others to digest the information.</p>
<p>For example, take your shopping list.</p>
<p>The order of the categories on the list matters. You'd want to order them based on the shop's layout. Ordering the categories based on the location near other categories would make sense. As this would make it quicker.</p>
<p>If the order of the categories were random, then the person reading the list would be less efficient in picking up items:</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-shopping-list-order.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="842" alt="The Shopping List as a pyramid: Go to Tesco for weekly shop at 7pm on Wednesday at the top, then Fresh fruit, Fresh vegetables and Dairy selection in order, each with its items beneath" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>Barbara Minto states that all mental processes (thinking, remembering, problem-solving) use this grouping and summarising process to identify patterns.</p>
<p>These groups and summaries form the first two levels of the pyramid in the human mind.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-mind-structure.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="845" alt="The Pyramid Mind Structure: three Key Insights, each summarising a group of data and information beneath it" loading="lazy">
</figure>

<h3>⏬ Ordering from the top-down</h3>
<p>The clearest communication presents information from the top down.</p>
<p>The human mind can only take in ideas one at a time. No matter how smart the audience, they will always have limited mental energy to process your ideas. So, it's important to make sure they spend the least mental effort comprehending a set of ideas.</p>
<p>So, controlling the sequence in which ideas are given is critical to get the point across. The clearest communication is always giving the summarised idea before giving the details.</p>
<p>You start with the main point, provide key insights and end with the details:</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-top-down-order.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1201" alt="Ordering from the top-down: start at the Main Takeaway, move through Key Insights 1 to 3, and end with the data and information at the bottom" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>Let's use the shopping list as an example.</p>
<p>You would start by communicating the main point: shopping needs to be done by a time and date. Then, state the categories of food needed (ordered by location). And finish by explaining the items needed from each category:</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-shopping-list-pyramid.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="842" alt="The Shopping List pyramid: the main point Go to Tesco for weekly shop at 7pm on Wednesday, then the categories in store order, then the items in each category" loading="lazy">
</figure>

<h2>⚙️ How does The Pyramid Principle work?</h2>
<p>The Pyramid Principle is split into two parts:</p>
<ol>
  <li><strong>Top-down thinking</strong> - Identifying a problem and a hypothesis before identifying the components that will help solve the problem.</li>
  <li><strong>Bottom-up thinking</strong> - Identifying the components of any system with data and determining what you can do with them.</li>
</ol>

<h3>🔽 Top-down thinking</h3>
<p>Every document or presentation <strong><em>must solve a problem by answering a key question</em></strong>.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<ul>
  <li>How can we grow the SEO visibility of our website?</li>
  <li>Why is our website seeing YoY declines in SEO traffic?</li>
  <li>What are our SEO competitors doing to rank for X keyword?</li>
</ul>
<p>The writer or presenter must use top-down thinking to identify the problem and key question.</p>
<p>Top-down thinking involves identifying a problem and a hypothesis before identifying a solution. In other words, you start with the end in mind and identify the components to make it happen.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-top-down-thinking.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="976" alt="Top-down thinking: arrows run from a single box at the top down to three boxes and then to smaller component boxes beneath each" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>How do you identify the question and problem?</p>
<p>By using the <strong>SCQA framework</strong>.</p>
<p>SCQA stands for <strong><em>Situation, Complication, Question and Answer.</em></strong> Barbara Minto created it to help identify the key question a document or presentation needs to answer.</p>
<p>Each statement in the framework helps define the context of the problem and the key question that needs to be answered. The framework also makes sure that the answer, supporting arguments, and data fit within the rules of constructing a pyramid.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-scqa-table.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1302" height="538" alt="SCQA table. Situation: a statement on the subject the reader already knows about. Complication: a fact-based description of the issue that raises the key question. Question: the key question that emerges from the situation and complication. Answer: the key takeaway that answers the question" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>The situation and complication statements help a consultant understand the business problem. And allow them to clarify the question before doing any research or analysis.</p>
<p><strong>This means the SCQA framework is not optional</strong> when using the Pyramid Principle.</p>
<p>You must use the SCQA framework to make sure you understand the problem and main question in your mind. If it isn't clear, you'll waste your time doing research and analysis that completely misses the mark.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-scqa-pyramid.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1201" alt="SCQA and Pyramid Structure: define the situation, complication and question before you write the answer, which then sits above three key insights and their data" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>Once you have defined the problem and question, you can start to identify solutions. This is where bottom-up thinking can help identify a potential answer to the key question.</p>

<h3>🔼 Bottom-up thinking</h3>
<p>Every document or presentation must <strong><em>answer the key question</em></strong>.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<ul>
  <li>How can we grow the SEO visibility of our B2B website?
    <ul><li><em>To grow organic traffic for our B2B website, we must build content hubs around key product topics.</em></li></ul>
  </li>
  <li>Why is our website seeing YoY declines in SEO traffic?
    <ul><li><em>Several Google core algorithm updates have affected our website over the last 12 months, and we need to invest in UX, content, and technical initiatives to reverse the decline.</em></li></ul>
  </li>
  <li>What are our SEO competitors doing to rank for revenue-driving keywords?
    <ul><li><em>Our competitors are investing in content that's driving links from authoritative websites, and we need to invest in similar activities to compete for revenue-driving keywords.</em></li></ul>
  </li>
</ul>
<p>The writer or presenter must use bottom-up thinking to identify the answer to the question based on the available data and information.</p>
<p>Bottom-up thinking starts with data and building up key themes or ideas to solve a problem. In other words, you start with the components and figure out what you can do.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-bottom-up-thinking.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="976" alt="Bottom-up thinking: arrows run up from small component boxes to three summary boxes and then to a single box at the top" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>But how do you find the answer to the question and provide supporting arguments?</p>
<p>By doing <strong>research/analysis</strong> and using the <strong>MECE principle.</strong></p>
<p>The acronym MECE stands for <strong><em>Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive</em></strong>. It's a grouping principle developed by Barbara Minto to help separate data/info into key insights. And a method for mapping complex ideas into the best sequence that anyone can understand.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-mece.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1214" alt="Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive (MECE): overlapping ideas are not exclusive, separate ideas are mutually exclusive, and a set of ideas that leaves gaps is not exhaustive, while one that covers everything is collectively exhaustive" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>This grouping and summarising principle helps consultants construct an answer and supporting arguments using data.</p>
<p>It's important to understand that the MECE principle <strong>is not a law</strong>. It's a means to an end.</p>
<p>But, Barbara Minto argues in her book that the pyramid needs to follow these three grouping and summarising rules to be effective:</p>
<ol>
  <li>Ideas at any level must be summaries of ideas grouped below them.</li>
  <li>Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea.</li>
  <li>Ideas must always be logically ordered.</li>
</ol>
<p>When writing or presenting to an audience, you must ensure that research and data are always grouped and summarised into key insights. Then, you summarise these insights into one clear answer to the document's key question.</p>
<p>Then, you present from the top down in your document or presentation.</p>
<p>If you find info/data that doesn't help answer the question while grouping and summarising, you exclude it from your document or presentation.</p>
<p>To keep your communication clear.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-mece-pyramid.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1201" alt="MECE and Pyramid Structure: similar data is grouped under three arguments, the arguments are summarised into the answer to the SCQA question, and info that doesn't fit is excluded" loading="lazy">
</figure>

<h2>🍫 Examples of the Pyramid Principle in Action</h2>
<p>Management and strategy consultants use the Pyramid Principle in many different ways:</p>
<ol>
  <li><strong>Persuasive Presentations</strong> - Create slide decks that help make business decisions.</li>
  <li><strong>Persuasive Slides</strong> - Create slides within presentations that are easy to digest.</li>
  <li><strong>Documents</strong> - Write emails, memos and briefs that persuade business teams to act.</li>
</ol>
<p>Below are a few examples of the technique in action.</p>

<h3>📽️ Persuasive Presentations</h3>
<p>The Pyramid Principle helps consultants build persuasive presentations.</p>
<p>An example is the "<a href="http://static2.snowfire.io/accounts/14796/files/219.pdf?t=o4z026" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Corporate Ventures in Sweden</a>" from the Boston Consulting Group (BCG).</p>
<p>The presentation includes the following:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Title:</strong> This states the key question the presentation is answering.</li>
  <li><strong>Executive Summary:</strong> This states the situation, complication and answer.</li>
  <li><strong>Key Insight:</strong> The summarised and grouped data/info to support the answer.</li>
  <li><strong>Appendix:</strong> The data and information that the key insights and answer is based on.</li>
</ul>
<p>The presentation communicates from the top down to answer the question, <em>"How can we improve the connection between Swedish startups and corporations?"</em>.</p>
<p>It uses the grouping and summarising principle to answer the question. And includes the data in the appendix. Which makes the presentation easier for the audience to digest.</p>

<p>The slides provide the main point in the slide title, group and summarise the key points, and then provide supporting details. This is the same structure used to build an entire presentation.</p>
<p>The slides get their point across by using the top-down technique. And help the audience use as little energy as possible to understand the point the slide is trying to make.</p>

<p>Like the slides, the documents get to the point using the top-down approach. And can be understood by the reader using as little brain power as possible.</p>

<h2>✍️ How to use the Pyramid Principle?</h2>
<p>A consultant using the Pyramid Principle will present ideas in the following three steps:</p>
<ol>
  <li>🧪 Define the problem and key question</li>
  <li>🧱 Write and structure your insights</li>
  <li>🗣️ Communicate the main takeaway and insights</li>
</ol>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-how-to-use.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1309" height="1201" alt="How to use the Pyramid Principle in three parts: Part 1, define the problem and key question; Part 2, write to structure your insights; Part 3, present back to stakeholders" loading="lazy">
</figure>

<h3>#1 Define the problem and key question</h3>
<p>Let's say the consultant needed to put together an SEO strategy for an enterprise website.</p>
<p>This SEO strategy must be presented to higher management and get buy-in from many teams. And the consultant wanted to use the pyramid to communicate.</p>
<p>They'd start by creating a brand new document.</p>
<p>Then, at the top of the page, define the situation and complication, which helps to define the problem the business faces. And a way to provide context and clarity for the strategy.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-situation-complication.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1282" height="816" alt="Situation and Complication written for a currency conversion product: organic traffic has grown since a rebrand and migration, but the product now needs to help grow multi-currency account customers by 50% (2.5 million new accounts) in 12 months" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>From the situation and complication, they then identify the key question that will help them overcome the business problem.</p>
<p>This isn't always easy. It sometimes takes time to find the right question that needs to be answered.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-key-question.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1272" height="420" alt="The key question: how can the currency conversion product contribute toward the goal of an extra 2.5 million personal multi-currency account customers through SEO traffic in the next 12 months?" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>Once you've identified the situation, complication and question, a consultant will then use data and insights to answer the question.</p>

<h3>#2 Write and structure your insights</h3>
<p>A consultant now needs to do research and analysis to structure their insights.</p>
<p>They'd use data and information to identify an answer to the key question that the document or presentation must answer. They then use the MECE principle to group and summarise the data into 3-5 key insights. And the summary of these ideas will form the answer.</p>
<p>A consultant dumps their data, information and raw ideas into the notes section of the document. Trying to identify relevant ideas that will help answer the question. This requires speaking to other teams, digging into analytic data and making sure ideas are valid.</p>
<p>They summarise and label their notes once they have relevant data and valid ideas.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-labelled-notes.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1322" height="1300" alt="A labelled note: reducing low-quality pages can help SEO traffic to the currency converter pages, supported by index coverage data, a quote from Google's documentation on low-quality content, and case study links" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>The consultant then summarises and groups labelled information into logical categories. These categories can then be used to form 3-5 key insights to answer the question.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-key-insights.webp' | relative_url }}" width="639" height="709" alt="Three key insights written under the Answer heading: the currency converter product can help drive a wider business goal, aggressive SEO investment could deliver 232,000 extra sign-ups, and competitor analysis shows content gaps to close" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>A consultant will then summarise the key insights into the answer to the key question. This will solve the business problem highlighted at the beginning of the process.</p>
<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-answer.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1302" height="368" alt="The answer: the currency converter product can contribute to the wider business goal, but it requires aggressive investment in the content and technical quality of its page templates" loading="lazy">
</figure>
<p>This process of labelling, summarising and grouping isn't quick.</p>
<p>It can take days or weeks to clarify the problem and key question. Only then does the consultant analyse the data to identify an answer.</p>

<h3>#3 Communicate the main takeaway and key insights</h3>
<p>A consultant will need to present the findings to higher management.</p>
<p>They must turn the written document into a presentation understood by busy managers.</p>
<p>The consultant has already written their ideas into a logical pyramid structure, so building a presentation is simple.</p>
<p>The consultant starts by stating the situation, complication and question in one slide. This sets the context and communicates back the brief to the audience. To make it clear, the assignment has been understood.</p>
<p>Then, build the executive summary. This section answers the question and provides the supporting arguments in 5-10 slides. It's this section that makes or breaks the presentation.</p>
<p><strong>The work in steps 1 and 2 ensures the presentation is bulletproof.</strong></p>
<p>Finally, the appendix contains all the data and insights on which the answer and supporting arguments are based. Further details can be found here if needed.</p>

  <img src="{{ '/assets/blog/pyramid-principle-presenting-findings.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1098" height="1600" alt="Presenting your findings: a context of work slide, then an executive summary that answers the question, then three summarised supporting arguments, each backed by appendix data" loading="lazy">
</figure>

<h3>#1 Getting buy-in for your SEO strategy</h3>
<p>Getting clients to buy into an SEO strategy is the perfect use of the Pyramid Principle.</p>
<p>An SEO strategy is always complex and messy to put together. It's even harder to communicate clearly to a room full of busy teams who don't care about the details. Especially when clients don't know the problem or question they want to solve.</p>
<p>The Pyramid Principle allows you to structure your recommendations in a way that can be easily understood by a non-technical audience.</p>

<h2>✅ The Pyramid Principle and SEO Communication</h2>
<p>Learning to communicate is a key skill to getting things done in SEO.</p>
<p>The Pyramid Principle is a technique that can communicate complex ideas to busy teams. It's one of the most popular and effective ways to get your message across because:</p>
<ul>
  <li>The mind will automatically group information into pyramid structures.</li>
  <li>Ideas communicated to an audience are easier to understand if sorted into pyramids.</li>
  <li>Any document or presentation is easier to understand if structured into a pyramid.</li>
</ul>
<p>SEO experts must combine top-down and bottom-up thinking to present ideas to non-technical teams.</p>
